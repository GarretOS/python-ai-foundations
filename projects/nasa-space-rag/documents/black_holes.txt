Title: Black Holes
Source: NASA Science — Black Holes
Source URL: https://science.nasa.gov/universe/black-holes/

A black hole is a huge concentration of matter packed into a very small space. Its gravity is so strong that, just beneath its event horizon, nothing can escape—not even light. The event horizon is not a solid surface like the surface of Earth or the Sun. It is a boundary containing the matter that makes up the black hole. NASA notes that black holes are still not fully understood, including what matter is like inside this boundary.

Black holes do not emit or reflect light, so telescopes cannot normally see them directly. Scientists instead study how black holes affect nearby matter and light. A black hole may be surrounded by an accretion disk, a ring of gas and dust that emits light at many wavelengths, including X-rays. Astronomers can also infer a supermassive black hole from the particular orbits of stars near it.

Other observations can reveal black holes as well. When massive objects accelerate through space, they can create gravitational waves—ripples in space-time that detectors may measure. A black hole’s gravity can also bend and distort light from more distant objects; this effect is called gravitational lensing and can help scientists find isolated black holes that would otherwise remain invisible. Black holes do not act like cosmic vacuum cleaners: from far away, their gravitational effects are like those of other objects with the same mass.
